# Exercise 03 — FDE vs. Adjacent Customer-Facing Roles

**Estimated effort:** 1 hour
**Output:** one role-comparison table + three scenario responses

## Objective

Internalise Chapter 04 by distinguishing the Forward Deployed Engineer from each adjacent customer-facing role in concrete scenarios. By the end, when a scope-creep request lands on your desk during a real engagement, you should recognise within seconds which adjacent role the request belongs to and route it correctly.

## Prerequisites

- You have read Chapter 04.
- You have read [`PREREQUISITES.md`](../../../PREREQUISITES.md), specifically the "Not in scope" section.

## Steps

1. **Build the comparison table.** For each of the eight adjacent roles in Chapter 04 (AE, pre-sales SE, CSM, CSE, Professional Services, Partner Engineer, DevRel, TAM — Field CTO optional), fill five cells:
   - Primary goal (one short phrase).
   - Primary artifact.
   - Where you touch them during a pilot (one sentence).
   - The sharp boundary (one sentence).
   - A telltale sign you have drifted into their role (one sentence).
2. **Respond to three scenarios.** For each of the three scenarios below, in 100–150 words, write: (a) which adjacent role this is really asking you to be, (b) how you would respond in the moment, (c) who you would route to, (d) what durable artifact you would produce so this does not recur.

   **Scenario A — The quiet quota.** Your AE is on PTO for ten days. The customer's sponsor sends you a message: "We're preparing our Q2 budget — can you just send me a price for another 100 seats of the pilot in the next week so we can close this before we lock the number?"

   **Scenario B — The weekend demo.** A new prospective customer is coming in next Thursday — not your design partner, a completely unrelated opportunity. The AE pings you: "Can you throw together a 30-minute demo on their data? You've done this before, I'll owe you one."

   **Scenario C — The public post.** A pilot you shipped two months ago has shown an impressive outcome (triage queue down 60%). Marketing asks you to write the engineering blog post, naming the customer, with a "lessons learned" section that happens to also include an open-source helper library you wrote during the pilot.

3. **Write the "I am not" statement.** In 150–200 words, write a statement to yourself titled "I am not the AE, SE, CSM, PS consultant, Partner Engineer, or DevRel." Each adjacent role gets one sentence naming the behaviour you will *not* drift into. Save it; you will use it in Project 104.

## Starter guidance

- The scenarios are deliberately designed to feel reasonable in the moment. They are the exact requests that cause well-meaning FDEs to drift into one of the adjacent roles. The point is not that the requests are bad — it is that the right response is often "yes but through the right channel," not "yes, right now."
- If a scenario answer is "I would just say no" without a route, you are under-answering. The customer and the asking colleague both deserve a path.
- Imagine reading your "I am not" statement to yourself on a Friday afternoon six months into your first FDE role. If it would not keep you honest, make it sharper.

## Acceptance criteria

- The comparison table covers all eight (or nine, with Field CTO) adjacent roles with five cells each.
- Each scenario response identifies the role correctly, proposes an in-the-moment reply, names the route, and names a durable artifact.
- The "I am not" statement reads cleanly and names a specific behaviour-to-avoid for each adjacent role.

## Stretch goals

- Write a fourth scenario yourself based on a situation you have seen (or can imagine) that is not covered by A/B/C. Respond to it in the same format.
- Find one line in your current employer's role library (or in a public AI company's job postings) where the boundaries between FDE and an adjacent role are blurred or ambiguous. Describe how you would interpret the ambiguity for yourself before you took the role.
