# Chapter 05 — Handling Customer-Side Resistance — Radical Transparency and Pragmatic Respect

## Why this chapter exists

Every meaningful pilot generates resistance from the customer side. The SME who has spent fifteen years adjudicating benefit-interpretation cases does not light up when a model begins drafting the first pass. The department director whose headcount budget is pressured by every piece of software the vendor ships does not send three reps to a weekly review out of enthusiasm. The IT reviewer who absorbed the aftermath of a prior vendor's security incident does not treat the next security questionnaire as a formality. In each case the resisting party is doing their job: domain experts protect quality, organisations protect their workflows, IT protects the enterprise. An FDE who reads the resistance as "the customer is being difficult" has already lost the pilot.

[Chapter 01](./01-consulting-craft-overlay.md) named resistance handling as one of the five consulting-craft dimensions. It is an optimisation dimension rather than a gate — a pilot can pass its weekly review cadence and its hand-off readiness with uneven resistance handling — but the pilots that survive their renewal conversation almost always have an FDE who treated the resisting stakeholders as the most important people in the room. Technical excellence does not substitute for this. The exec demo that lands with the VP does nothing for a pilot that the SME lead never championed inside the operating function.

This chapter writes the FDE's stance, three resistance archetypes with signal/diagnosis/response, the specific techniques, the escalation pathway when resistance persists, sample dialogues, and a respect-signalling glossary. It cross-references [mod-108](../mod-108-enterprise-security-and-compliance/) for the IT-review response, [mod-110 Chapter 06](../mod-110-observability-and-oncall/06-blameless-postmortems-and-eval-backfeed.md) for the failure-walkthrough posture, and [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-fde-discipline-and-position/04-adjacent-customer-facing-roles.md) for the role boundaries the FDE must not cross when managing customer politics.

A single framing note before the content. Resistance is not evenly distributed across a pilot's timeline. The archetype-A SME is usually quiet in weeks 1–2 (goodwill, novelty) and loud in weeks 4–8 (first failures, first workflow friction). The archetype-B department is usually quiet in weeks 1–3 (pilot has not reached the department's rhythm yet) and loud in weeks 6–10 (go-live window is visible on the calendar). The archetype-C IT review is usually loud in weeks 1–2 (initial questionnaire), quiet in weeks 3–8 (the FDE thinks the review is done), and loud again in weeks 10–12 (pre-go-live architecture sign-off). The FDE who calendars for "resistance will land evenly" will be surprised; the FDE who calendars for the archetype-specific curve will not.

## The FDE's stance — radical transparency plus pragmatic respect

Two stances, held simultaneously. Neither is optional under schedule pressure.

**Radical transparency** means the FDE never hides a failure, a limitation, or a ship-blocker from any customer-side stakeholder who has a legitimate interest in knowing. The SME finds out about the model's hallucinated citation in the Thursday pre-meeting 1-on-1, before the Friday weekly review, from the FDE — not three weeks later, from a member complaint that escalates to the clinical review committee. The IT reviewer hears about the open logging gap on the telemetry pipeline when the FDE opens the working session, not when the reviewer discovers it themselves and concludes the FDE was managing them. The sponsor learns that the pilot's week-6 KPI is amber because the pilot is routing on a dataset the SME lead now disagrees with the shape of — not from a theatre-green status slide.

Radical transparency is structural, not stylistic. It is the practice that earns the FDE the right to ask customer-side people to change their workflow, trust the pilot's outputs, or accept a limitation as a known-and-bounded one rather than a hidden one. An FDE who hides yellow once will find, on the next yellow, that nobody believes the green.

**Pragmatic respect** means naming the SME's craft, the department's institutional knowledge, and the IT reviewer's constraints — without condescension, without strategic flattery. The clinical reviewer is not "the customer SME we need to bring along"; they are the person who has read ten thousand benefit-interpretation cases and whose tacit knowledge the pilot is attempting to encode. The department director is not "a political obstacle"; they are the person responsible for a team whose jobs are plausibly affected by the pilot's outcome and who has a duty of care toward them. The IT reviewer is not "slowing us down"; they are the person whose signature will be on the ATO or the architecture sign-off and who will own the consequences if something goes wrong.

Pragmatic respect is also structural. It is not "being polite." Politeness is cheap and transparently manipulable. Respect shows up in the vocabulary the FDE uses in the exec brief, in which artefacts the FDE cites when making a point, in whose judgement the FDE defers to on a domain call, and in whether the FDE uses the word "automate" when "augment" is the honest word.

The two stances compound. Radical transparency without respect reads as cold and often as blame-shifting — the FDE who publishes every failure on a dashboard without having prepared the SME in advance has not actually earned transparency; they have surprised the SME in public. Respect without transparency reads as management theatre — the FDE who flatters the SME's expertise but hides the evaluation result where the model disagreed with the SME's call has not actually respected the SME; they have patronised them.

### What radical transparency is not

It is not public shaming of the model, the team, or the FDE. It is not a dashboard that lists every eval regression in front of an audience that has no context for interpreting them. It is not a confessional posture in the weekly review where the FDE spends the status slot cataloguing concerns. Transparency has a shape: the right stakeholder, the right time, the right level of detail, the right framing. The 1-on-1 is almost always the right first channel; the group review is almost always the second. Written-first (minutes, exec brief) is usually third — never the first place a resisting stakeholder learns about a failure that affects them.

### What pragmatic respect is not

It is not agreement. The FDE can respect an SME's craft and disagree with their technical read of a model output; the disagreement is held in the open, with evidence, in the artefact. Respect is not a mode of yielding. It is a mode of taking the other person seriously enough to argue with them on the merits. An FDE who defers on every domain call has not respected the SME — they have abdicated the engineering stance the pilot requires.

## Three resistance archetypes

### Archetype A — the SME who prefers the legacy workflow

**Signals.** "This is faster when I just do it myself." "The model missed a nuance I'd catch." "I don't have time to review the draft this week." Silent non-engagement — the SME attends the weekly review but does not contribute, or sends a delegate who cannot answer domain questions. Participation that was high in weeks 1–2 declines sharply by week 4. The gold-set additions the SME committed to stop landing. Second-order signals: the SME's team members, who previously tried the pilot's drafts, revert to the legacy tool and stop logging drafts for review; informal corridor comments (relayed through the sponsor or the ops lead) that "the model isn't really up to this"; the SME's calendar invites to pilot meetings sit on "tentative" until the morning of the meeting.

**Diagnosis.** Three possibilities, often mixed.

(i) *A real quality gap the model has that the SME has caught.* The SME is right. The model is missing a nuance the SME's craft routinely catches. This is the best possible outcome of the resistance — the SME is giving the FDE a free evaluation signal — and the FDE's response must recognise it as such. The appropriate response is to backfeed the failure into the gold set, treat the SME as the authority on the domain call, and show the SME at the next review the eval delta the failure produced.

(ii) *A familiarity cost plus workflow-switching friction.* The legacy workflow is encoded in muscle memory; the pilot's UI, prompt template, or review-queue shape is unfamiliar; the switching cost is real and under-acknowledged. The SME is not opposed to the pilot in principle but is paying a tax every time they use it. The appropriate response is to shadow the SME through the legacy workflow for a session, identify the specific friction points, and either fix the UI/workflow or acknowledge the tax explicitly so it does not become grievance.

(iii) *A role threat.* The SME believes, correctly or incorrectly, that a successful pilot reduces their standing, their headcount prospects, or the perceived value of their craft. This is the hardest diagnosis and the one FDEs most frequently misread. The appropriate response is not an FDE-authored reassurance — the FDE does not have the authority to make role-preservation commitments. The appropriate response is to surface the signal to the sponsor and convene a sponsor + SME-director conversation. See the escalation pathway below.

**Response.** Start by assuming (i). The SME's "the model missed a nuance" is the most valuable sentence they will say all month. Ask for the specific case, log it in the gold set, and make it a running agenda item. If after two cycles of closed-loop fixes the resistance has not softened, consider (ii) — shadow the workflow, propose a UI fix, name the switching tax out loud. If after that the resistance has still not softened, consider (iii) and route to the escalation pathway.

The order matters. FDEs who jump to diagnosis (iii) first — "the SME feels threatened" — frequently discover afterwards that the model actually was missing a nuance, that the SME was right, and that the FDE's premature pivot to political framing compounded the problem. The quality-gap diagnosis is cheap to test (ask for the case, run the retrieval, inspect the trace), it almost always teaches the FDE something useful even when it is not the primary cause, and it signals respect for the SME's craft in a way that the political framing cannot.

### Archetype B — the department that views the pilot as a threat

**Signals.** The department director declines to send reps to the weekly review, or sends an ops manager with no decision authority. The ops lead "doesn't have bandwidth" for the co-design sessions the FDE scheduled. Roadmap questions the FDE sends to the department go unanswered for two weeks. The pilot's go-live window is scheduled during the department's off-site. Senior ICs in the department express mild enthusiasm in 1-on-1s and silence in the group review. Second-order signals: the department's quarterly planning deck does not mention the pilot even where the pilot's use-case is clearly in-scope; the department's union or employee resource group has quietly scheduled an all-hands that the FDE finds out about only afterward; the sponsor's requests for metrics from the department come back in a format that cannot be joined to the pilot's telemetry.

**Diagnosis.** The pilot is being read as workforce reduction. The director may or may not have been told explicitly that the pilot is about headcount efficiency; often they have not been told either way, and the ambiguity reads, correctly, as threat. The FDE does *not* have authority to make role-preservation commitments to the department — that is a sponsor-level and often a C-level decision. The FDE's job is to surface the political read to the sponsor, honestly, and let the sponsor decide whether and how to address it.

**Response.** The FDE does not try to resolve this alone. Resolving it alone almost always means the FDE making a vague reassurance ("this isn't about headcount") that is either untrue or unauthorised; both outcomes end the pilot. The response has three parts:

1. **Name the pattern in the sponsor 1-on-1.** The FDE tells the sponsor: "I think the department is reading the pilot as a workforce reduction signal. Here are the three signals that led me there. I don't have authority to address that directly. I think the right next step is a conversation between you and [director]."
2. **Request a sponsor + director conversation.** The sponsor either does this (which usually unblocks the pilot) or declines (which tells the FDE a great deal about the pilot's political footing).
3. **Keep the FDE's hands clean of the HR narrative.** The FDE does not promise jobs, does not promise redeployment, does not promise augmentation-not-automation beyond what the sponsor has authorised in writing. Pragmatic respect here means not making commitments the FDE does not own.

There is a secondary move available: the FDE can offer to make the department director a visible co-owner of the pilot's outcomes — a named contributor on the exec brief, an attendee at the executive demo, a signatory on the hand-off readiness gate. Co-ownership is cheap to extend and often reframes the pilot from "a thing being done to the department" to "a thing being done with the department." It is not a substitute for the sponsor + director conversation; it is a complement to it.

### Archetype C — the IT review that feels adversarial

**Signals.** The security questionnaire expands scope mid-review — new sections appear, old answers get re-asked. The architecture-review timeline doubles between the first and second meeting. The architecture asks do not match the product the FDE has actually shipped (requests for on-prem inference when the pilot is cloud-hosted; requests for a bespoke audit log format when the enterprise logging pipeline already ingests the vendor's format). The IT reviewer brings a vendor comparison to the meeting — unprompted, and often citing a competitor the FDE has not heard the customer mention. Second-order signals: the ticketing system shows the pilot's security tickets getting re-queued from one reviewer to another; the reviewer's calendar for a working session is booked out four weeks (the FDE should read this as either genuine load or signal, not personal); the reviewer begins cc-ing their manager on every email, which is often the reviewer looking for cover rather than looking for escalation.

**Diagnosis.** Three possibilities, in rough order of frequency.

(i) *Legitimate concern the FDE has not surfaced.* Most common. The IT reviewer has caught something real — a logging gap, a data-residency question, an unclear retention policy, a missing SOC 2 control mapping. The adversarial feel is partly the reviewer's style and partly frustration that these questions did not come up earlier. The appropriate response is to acknowledge the concern, name what the FDE knows and does not know, and schedule a working session to close the gap jointly.

(ii) *Political opposition inside IT.* Less common but real. Someone in the IT organisation has a reason — a preferred alternative vendor, a prior relationship, a view that the pilot's sponsor is encroaching on IT's territory — to want the pilot to fail. This is not usually malicious; it is institutional. The appropriate response is to find the IT champion (there almost always is one) and to make the pilot's architecture easy for the champion to defend.

(iii) *Scar tissue from a prior bad vendor.* The IT organisation has been burned — a SaaS vendor that leaked data, an AI pilot elsewhere that bypassed IT review entirely, an audit finding attributable to an insufficiently reviewed deployment. The adversarial posture is defensive, not personal. The appropriate response is to acknowledge the history where the FDE knows it, lean hard into the transparency stance, and over-document.

**Response.** In all three cases the response begins the same way:

- **Schedule a working session**, not a status update. Co-walk the open questions. Bring the mod-108 security-posture one-pager. Bring the pilot's architecture diagram with data-flow annotations. Bring the retention and logging posture in writing.
- **Find the IT champion.** Someone inside the IT organisation is willing to help the pilot succeed — usually an architect or a senior engineer who has seen enough pilots to distinguish the serious vendors from the SE-prototype ones. Invest in that relationship.
- **Invite the IT reviewer to the weekly pilot review as a standing observer.** This inverts the adversarial read. The reviewer stops being an external gate and starts being a participant with early visibility into what the pilot is doing. It is cheap, it costs the FDE one additional recipient on the minutes, and it almost always reduces the surface area of the formal review.
- **Never end-run around IT.** The temptation — going to the sponsor to "unblock" IT review — is a pilot-ending anti-pattern. See the anti-patterns section.

The IT-review response has one additional subtlety that distinguishes it from archetypes A and B. IT reviewers often operate on a different time horizon than the FDE: the reviewer has seen enough pilots fail at year-two for architecture reasons that the current pilot's week-6 schedule pressure is, from the reviewer's perspective, the wrong framing. The FDE who hears "we need another three weeks on data-residency review" and responds with "we're on a tight timeline" has lost the room. The FDE who responds with "understood — let's scope the three weeks precisely so I can plan the pilot calendar around it" has earned a partner.

## Specific techniques

**The pre-meeting 1-on-1 with the SME lead.** Fifteen minutes, standing, every week, before the group pilot review. Never skipped. The agenda is never written down formally; the content varies. What is non-negotiable is that the SME lead walks into the group review having already heard any surprise the FDE would otherwise deliver in front of the sponsor, the IT liaison, and the ops lead. The 1-on-1 is where radical transparency is operationalised — the SME finds out about the model's week-6 regression in the Thursday 1-on-1, not in the Friday group review.

The standing 1-on-1 is also where the FDE collects the week's informal signal: which team members have been complaining about the pilot in the SME's hearing, which cases the SME flagged but did not bring to the formal review, which part of the roadmap the SME would quietly redirect if they had the authority. The FDE does not act on this signal unilaterally; the FDE uses it to inform the next week's prep and, when relevant, the sponsor 1-on-1. The 1-on-1 is a listening posture more than a telling posture — the FDE talks second, not first.

**The co-design session.** The SME becomes a shared author of the prompt template, the evaluation rubric, and the gold set — not a reviewer of the FDE's drafts but a named contributor to the artefacts. The co-design shows up in the artefacts themselves: "Prompt template v3.2 — co-authored with [SME name]"; "Gold-set entries 47–89 sourced from [SME name]'s case archive." The respect this signals is not performative — it is structural. The SME can point at the artefact in their own performance review and say "I contributed to this."

The co-design session has a specific shape. Thirty to sixty minutes, screen-share, one artefact per session (prompt template, rubric, or gold-set batch — not all three in one meeting). The FDE opens with the current artefact and the three decisions the FDE needs the SME's input on, not the full set of possible changes. The SME drives the discussion on the three; the FDE edits in real time. At the end the FDE commits the artefact with the SME's name on the change. The session's output is the commit, not a document. That shape — small, scoped, outputs a real artefact with the SME's name on it — is what distinguishes co-design from a review meeting.

**The failure walkthrough.** When the model fails on a case the SME cares about, the FDE shows the SME the failure before the SME finds it themselves. The walkthrough has a shape borrowed from [mod-110 Chapter 06](../mod-110-observability-and-oncall/06-blameless-postmortems-and-eval-backfeed.md): what happened, what the model did, why it did that, what the fix is, when the fix lands, what the fix changes about the SME's workflow. The walkthrough is blameless toward the model and toward the SME. It is not an apology — it is a shared investigation.

The walkthrough has one specific rule that distinguishes it from the mod-110 postmortem: the SME gets to add the case to the gold set during the walkthrough, with the SME's label of what the correct output would have been. That addition is the bridge from "the model failed" to "the model's failure made the eval better." The SME walks out of the walkthrough with a concrete contribution to the pilot's quality, not just a complaint logged. That is the difference between the walkthrough and a bug report, and it is why the walkthrough compounds trust rather than erodes it.

**The "explicit non-goal."** Name what the pilot will NOT replace. The explicit non-goal is almost always the thing the SME was most worried about. "The pilot will not draft final benefit determinations without clinical review" is a non-goal that an archetype-A SME will remember for the rest of the engagement. The non-goal lives in the exec brief, in the weekly review's standing agenda, and in the hand-off runbook. Naming it costs the FDE nothing and pays compound interest on trust.

The non-goals are live, not static. As the pilot runs, some non-goals will tighten (the model turned out to be worse at X than expected; the non-goal around X becomes more absolute) and some may loosen (the SME, after four months of co-design, signs off on a narrow class of outputs being produced without per-case review). Every change to the non-goals is a decision in the countersigned decision log. The FDE does not quietly expand scope by relaxing a non-goal between weekly reviews; doing so is one of the fastest ways to lose an SME who was previously a champion.

**Respect-signalling moves.** Use the SME's vocabulary — if they say "member" rather than "customer," so does the FDE; if they say "case" rather than "ticket," so does the FDE. Cite their artefacts in the exec brief. Defer to their judgement on domain calls, visibly, in group settings. Use the word "augment" when it is honest, and never use "automate" when "augment" is accurate.

**The shadow session.** Once per pilot, early (weeks 2–3), the FDE sits next to the SME for a full shift — four hours for most roles, a full day for high-volume operations roles. The FDE does not run the pilot during the shadow; the FDE watches the SME do their job. The output of the shadow is a two-page observation note the FDE sends to the SME for correction before sending it to the sponsor. The shadow produces three things: workflow-friction points the FDE did not previously see; vocabulary the FDE did not previously know to use; and an unmistakable signal that the FDE takes the craft seriously enough to spend four unbilled hours learning it.

**The retrospective invite.** When the pilot runs its week-6 or week-8 retrospective, the resisting SME is invited as a named participant, not as an optional attendee. Their observations are anchoring agenda items, not addenda. The retrospective's output names them as a contributor. Over three retrospectives, the SME's role in the pilot shifts from "person being asked to adopt" to "person shaping what adoption looks like." That shift is the pilot.

## When to escalate

If resistance persists past 4–6 weeks despite good-faith engagement — the pre-meeting 1-on-1s are happening, the co-design sessions have been offered, the failure walkthroughs have been shared, the non-goals are explicit — the FDE escalates. Two parallel channels: the customer sponsor, and the FDE's own `ai-engineering-manager`. See [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-fde-discipline-and-position/04-adjacent-customer-facing-roles.md) for how this interacts with the `customer-success-manager` relationship (the CSM may need to know, but is not the escalation owner — the sponsor is).

Escalation is socialised in 1-on-1s before it lands in writing. The FDE tells the sponsor in Thursday's prep 1-on-1 that they are about to escalate; the FDE tells their manager at the Friday internal review. Nobody is surprised by the escalation note. Surprise escalation ends relationships.

A sample escalation note, three to five sentences, lands by email or in a shared channel after the socialisation:

> Sponsor — following up on our 1-on-1 Thursday. The SME lead has declined the last three co-design sessions and sent a delegate without case-decision authority to the weekly review for four weeks running. I've tried the standard moves (pre-meeting 1-on-1s, failure walkthroughs, explicit non-goal in the exec brief) and the engagement has not shifted. I think we're past what I can resolve at the FDE level and would like to convene a sponsor + SME-director conversation this week. My manager [name] is aware and available to join if useful.

### Escalation hygiene

Three rules. Escalation is socialised in 1-on-1s before it is written. Escalation names what the FDE has already tried, in enough detail to show good faith, without being a litigation brief. Escalation requests a specific next step — a conversation, a decision, a reassignment — not a vague "we need to talk about this."

### What does not count as escalation

A note worth drawing a line around. Routine honest status — "the SME lead missed three co-design sessions; I'm going to try a pre-meeting 1-on-1 next week" — is not escalation, it is weekly-review hygiene (see [Chapter 04](./04-weekly-pilot-review-cadence.md)). Escalation is reserved for the case where the FDE has run out of moves at the FDE level and needs leadership action from either chain. Confusing routine honesty with escalation inflates escalation currency and makes real escalation harder to land when it is needed.

### When the sponsor is the resisting party

A rarer case but worth naming. Sometimes the resistance is at the sponsor level — the sponsor has quietly lost confidence in the pilot, delays decisions, stops reading the exec brief, misses the executive demo. The FDE's recourse here is their own `ai-engineering-manager`, not the sponsor's chain. The manager + sponsor conversation is the equivalent move — above the FDE's authority, requiring leadership-to-leadership framing. The FDE does not go around the sponsor to the sponsor's leadership; that violates the role boundaries in [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-fde-discipline-and-position/04-adjacent-customer-facing-roles.md) and ends the engagement regardless of the pilot's technical outcome.

## Sample dialogues

Six to eight turns each. Mundane phrasing. The customer-side column is the resisting party; the FDE column is the FDE.

|---|---|
| "I looked at the drafts the model produced this week. The one on case 4418 misread the plan document — it cited the wrong subsection." | "Thank you — can you walk me through what the model missed on 4418? I'd like to put it in the gold set." |
| "The section it cited was about cost-share. The actual question was about medical necessity. Those are different parts of the policy." | "Got it. So the model conflated two sections of the plan doc. Do you see this pattern elsewhere, or is 4418 a one-off?" |
| "I'd have to look. I've seen it maybe two other times in the last three weeks." | "If you can flag those when you see them, I'll run them against the retrieval chain and show you at Thursday's 1-on-1 what's happening upstream." |
| "This is why I'd rather just do these myself. It's faster than fixing what the model got wrong." | "Fair. Let me ask — if the model got the section-selection right on cases like 4418, does that change the arithmetic for you, or is there a different piece of the workflow that's still slower than doing it yourself?" |
| "If it got the section right, I'd still want to read it. But I wouldn't be rewriting it." | "That's the pilot's explicit non-goal — it doesn't produce final determinations without you. Can we schedule thirty minutes next week to co-design the section-selection prompt, with 4418 and the two others as the seed cases?" |
| "Thursday afternoon works." | "I'll send an invite. I'll also bring the eval delta at the Friday review so [sponsor] sees the change the co-design produces." |
| "One thing — I don't want this framed as me signing off on the model. I want it framed as the model learning a case I flagged." | "That's exactly how I'll frame it. The change note in the exec brief will say the prompt was co-designed off three cases you flagged. I'll send you the draft before it goes out." |

## Respect-signalling glossary

Six phrases that signal respect for the customer-side person's craft. None of these are flattery; each is a structural move the FDE can make inside a sentence.

- **"That's a domain call — I'd defer to you."** Used when the SME is adjudicating a judgement the model should not make unilaterally.
- **"I don't know — can you walk me through how you'd handle it?"** Used when the FDE genuinely does not know the domain shape, which is more often than FDEs usually admit.
- **"The pilot's explicit non-goal is X."** Names what the pilot will not do, usually the thing the SME was worried about.
- **"We'll co-author the rubric — your name goes on it."** Used when the gold set, prompt template, or evaluation rubric is being built.
- **"That's a concern I hadn't surfaced. Thank you for raising it before sign-off."** Used in IT and model-risk reviews. Treats the reviewer's catch as a positive contribution, not an obstacle.
- **"I'll bring that to my manager and to the sponsor — it's above my authority to commit on."** Used when a resisting party asks for something the FDE cannot promise alone.
- **"We'll be slower this sprint so we can integrate what you raised."** Used when the resisting party's input will delay a milestone. Names the trade-off out loud rather than hiding it in a status update.
- **"This is going in the hand-off runbook under your name — I'd like you to review the section."** Used late in the pilot when the artefact the SME has contributed to is being codified.

Two anti-phrases worth banning from the FDE's vocabulary. "Change management" — accurate as a discipline, poisonous as a word spoken at the SME; it reduces a person's craft to a logistical problem. "Buy-in" — betrays that the FDE has already decided the direction and is now managing the SME toward it. Replace both with naming the specific move the FDE is making and the specific authority under which they are making it.

### A note on dialogue craft

Three things make the sample dialogues above realistic rather than idealised. First, the FDE's turns are mostly short — one or two sentences — and they almost always end with either a question or a concrete next step, not a declarative. Second, the FDE names constraints and non-goals in the moment ("that's the pilot's explicit non-goal"; "it's above my authority to commit on") rather than hedging around them. Third, the FDE asks for the specific case, the specific standard, the specific document — not a vague "tell me more." Dialogue craft is not charisma; it is the discipline of short turns, named constraints, and specific asks.

## Running the three archetypes

**Archetype A — US mid-size healthcare payer.** The primary resistance split is between the clinical reviewer (quality-protection, archetype-A-type-(i)) and the member-service staff (workflow-fit, archetype-A-type-(ii)). The pre-meeting 1-on-1 with the clinical reviewer runs standing on Thursday afternoons and always carries a case example the reviewer picked the week before — the FDE arrives with the model's trace on that case, the gold-set entry if the case was added, and the eval delta if the case has driven a prompt or retrieval change. The member-service staff resistance is addressed by shadowing a representative for a full shift during week 3 and naming the two or three workflow friction points in the week-4 exec brief as pilot-side work, not member-service-side work. The clinical review committee is invited to the weekly pilot review as a standing attendee alongside the privacy office. <!-- needs-research: confirm the standard composition of a payer-side clinical review committee and whether their attendance at a pilot review requires a specific HIPAA or minimum-necessary review memo -->

The explicit non-goal that matters most for archetype A is "the pilot will not produce member-facing communications touching benefit interpretation without clinical sign-off." Named in week 1, repeated in the exec brief every month, cited in the hand-off runbook. If the non-goal evolves — if the clinical reviewer eventually signs off on a narrow class of determinations being produced without per-case review — that evolution is itself a decision in the countersigned decision log (see [Chapter 06](./06-scope-discipline-and-decision-log.md)), not a quiet shift the FDE makes between weekly reviews.

The archetype-A escalation pathway, if needed, routes through the sponsor's chain to the Head of Member Experience, then (rarely) to the Chief Operating Officer. The FDE's own chain parallels: the `ai-engineering-manager` is socialised at the Friday internal review, and the engineering director is looped in only if the pattern persists past 8 weeks. Escalations in archetype A are rare in practice; the clinical reviewer's resistance almost always resolves at diagnosis (i) within two co-design cycles when handled well.

**Archetype B — US federal agency.** FedRAMP agency resistance most often originates in two places: the mission-owner, who holds audit-trail concerns (how will the pilot's decisions be reconstructible for an inspector general review?), and the CISO's office, which holds model-provenance concerns (what model, which version, trained on what, hosted where, under what ATO boundary?). Both are legitimate and neither can be answered with marketing language. The FDE brings the pilot's decision log (from [Chapter 06](./06-scope-discipline-and-decision-log.md)) to the mission-owner conversation and the mod-108 security-posture one-pager plus the ATO boundary diagram to the CISO's office conversation. The IT reviewer invitation to the weekly review is especially high-leverage in archetype B — the enclave means the number of people who see the pilot's internals is small, and a standing CISO-office observer substantially reduces the formal-review surface area. <!-- needs-research: confirm which NIST AI RMF 1.0 governance functions map to the mission-owner's audit-trail concerns and whether OMB M-24-10 specifies the reconstructibility shape -->

Archetype B has a specific department-level resistance pattern worth naming. Career civil servants in the operating function often carry institutional memory of past technology initiatives that were announced as workforce-augmenting and later became workforce-reducing. The FDE does not dismiss this memory; it is almost always accurate at some level. The response is the archetype-B response above — surface to the sponsor, request the sponsor + director conversation — with the additional move of ensuring the pilot's exec brief contains, in writing, whatever role-preservation framing the sponsor has authorised. If no such framing has been authorised, the FDE does not invent one.

The archetype-B dynamic in the federal context is further complicated by the fact that the mission-owner and the department director may be the same person or may be two different people reporting into the same Assistant Secretary. Where they are two different people, the FDE treats both as first-class stakeholders — the mission-owner holds the audit-trail concern (archetype-C-like, around auditability), the department director holds the workforce concern (archetype-B proper). The sponsor + director conversation in this case may actually be a sponsor + mission-owner + director three-way, which the FDE requests but does not run.

**Archetype C — EU retail bank.** Resistance often originates in the model-risk function, which demands evidence that the pilot satisfies the bank's existing model-validation standard — usually a document family in the MV-STD-* numbering range, inherited from the bank's prior model-risk regime and overlaid with EU AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring obligations. The FDE's response begins by requesting the standard (never pretending to know it), mapping the pilot's evaluation posture against each standard section, and walking the model-risk function through the map in a working session. The model-risk function head is invited to the weekly pilot review as a standing co-attendee — not as an observer but as a participant — because in archetype C the model-risk function will sign the hand-off readiness gate alongside the sponsor. <!-- needs-research: confirm the typical reporting line of the model-risk function in EU retail banks (CRO vs CFO) and whether DORA ICT-third-party register entries are maintained by the model-risk function or a separate ICT-risk office -->

The co-attendance invitation is an archetype-C-specific move. In archetypes A and B the model-risk-equivalent function (clinical review committee, CISO's office) is typically invited as an observer rather than a co-participant because the function's authority runs through formal review rather than through weekly operating decisions. In archetype C, the model-risk function's authority runs through both — their weekly operating concerns become formal findings at the model-validation gate — so they belong in the weekly conversation as a first-class participant. The FDE who treats them as an observer loses them at the gate.

Archetype C also has a secondary resistance pattern worth naming: the bank's DORA ICT-third-party register entry for the pilot creates a formal exit obligation. The FDE's resistance work with the model-risk function must land a hand-off posture that satisfies the register's exit-plan requirement — specifically, a documented path by which the bank can run the pilot's workflow without the vendor. The FDE who neglects this will find the model-risk function declining to sign the hand-off readiness gate even when every evaluation metric is green, because the exit-plan evidence is missing. [Chapter 07](./07-knowledge-transfer-to-customer-engineering.md) covers the hand-off craft; the archetype-C exit-plan evidence is part of that. <!-- needs-research: confirm whether DORA ICT-third-party register entries have a specific exit-plan documentation schema or whether the bank's internal standard governs -->

Resistance in archetype C is also unusual in that it is often more professional and less personal than in archetypes A and B. The model-risk function treats the pilot as a model under their standard regime; the FDE who reads the professional tone as an absence of resistance will miss the real tension, which is that the model-risk function may be preparing a formal finding without having warned the FDE. The pre-meeting 1-on-1 with the model-risk lead — the equivalent of the SME 1-on-1 in archetype A — is how the FDE avoids being surprised by a written finding at the gate.

### Cross-archetype comparison

| Dimension | Archetype A (HIPAA payer) | Archetype B (FedRAMP agency) | Archetype C (EU retail bank) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant resisting function | Clinical reviewer + member-service staff | Mission-owner + department director | Model-risk function + IT security |
| Dominant concern | Clinical quality + workflow fit | Audit trail + workforce | Model validation + DORA exit |
| Primary technique | Case-driven co-design + failure walkthrough | Sponsor + director conversation; exec brief role-framing | Model-validation standard mapping + model-risk 1-on-1 |
| Non-goal that matters most | "No member-facing comms on benefit interpretation without clinical sign-off" | "No mission-affecting output without reconstructible audit trail" | "No model promotion without model-validation-standard sign-off" |
| Hand-off signatory (beyond sponsor) | Clinical review committee chair; privacy office | Mission-owner Assistant Secretary; CISO's office | Model-risk function head; DORA ICT-risk office |
| Typical escalation timing | Rare; resolves at co-design | Mid-pilot (weeks 6–10) around workforce framing | Late-pilot (weeks 10–14) around gate findings |

The table is not a template to be followed literally for every pilot; it is a reminder that the archetype shapes which technique has leverage and which stakeholder must be on the readiness gate. A move that lands with the clinical reviewer in archetype A may not land with the model-risk function in archetype C, not because the technique is wrong but because the function's authority runs through a different mechanism.

## How the techniques compose across a pilot's life

The techniques above are not a menu the FDE picks from in a single week; they compose across the pilot's life.

**Weeks 1–2 — set-up.** Standing pre-meeting 1-on-1 with the SME lead is on the calendar before the first weekly review. Explicit non-goals are named in the week-1 exec brief, co-authored with the SME. A shadow session is scheduled for week 2 or 3. The IT reviewer is invited to the weekly review as a standing observer.

**Weeks 3–6 — first failures.** The first failure walkthroughs land, delivered in the pre-meeting 1-on-1 before the group review. The first co-design session runs. The retrospective at week 6 has the SME as a named participant. Yellow is yellow in the exec brief.

**Weeks 7–10 — drift catch.** Resistance patterns that are going to emerge have usually emerged by now. The FDE runs the signal-diagnosis-response loop for whichever archetype is present. If a pattern persists past week 8 despite the standard moves, escalation is socialised in 1-on-1s and lands in writing by week 10.

**Weeks 11–16 — hand-off and gate.** The resisting stakeholder is a signatory on the hand-off readiness gate. If the gate signature is not forthcoming, the FDE has not resolved the resistance and the hand-off is not ready — regardless of how green the technical metrics are. See [Chapter 07](./07-knowledge-transfer-to-customer-engineering.md).

The compose-across-the-pilot framing matters because single-week moves rarely resolve resistance; cumulative moves across weeks do. The FDE's calendar is the mechanism by which the moves accumulate.

## A note on resistance from the FDE's own chain

Resistance is not only customer-side. The FDE's own `ai-engineering-manager`, product team, or platform team can be the resisting party — the manager who disagrees with the pilot's direction, the product team whose roadmap does not align with the SME's requested fix, the platform team that pushes back on an architectural choice the IT reviewer wants. This chapter is about customer-side resistance; vendor-side resistance is treated in [mod-111](../mod-111-field-to-product-loop/). But the stances carry: radical transparency with the customer requires that the FDE's own chain is not a surprise obstacle revealed at the weekly review. If the FDE's chain and the customer are on different pages, the FDE resolves it internally first, or names the disagreement in writing — never performs agreement in the customer meeting and renegotiates in private.

## Anti-patterns

**The FDE as salesperson.** The FDE tells the resisting SME "you're going to love this once you see it in action." The SME has heard this before, from vendors who were wrong. The stance drifts toward the `account-executive` role (see [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-fde-discipline-and-position/04-adjacent-customer-facing-roles.md)) and the FDE loses the technical credibility that is their actual asset. Replace with: naming the current failure, showing the eval delta, letting the artefact speak. The `solutions-engineer` demo stance is a related drift; the FDE is not selling the product to the SME, and conflating the two reads immediately as miscalibration.

**The hidden failure mode.** The FDE knows the model has a pattern of failing on a specific case shape; the FDE does not surface it to the SME because "it's being fixed this sprint." The SME finds it independently during sprint 2 and interprets the silence as management. Trust evaporates. Replace with: the failure walkthrough, before the SME finds it themselves. The rule is simple and non-negotiable: every failure the FDE knows about that materially affects the SME's domain or workflow gets surfaced to the SME within one weekly cycle, in the pre-meeting 1-on-1, with a plan — even a provisional one. Hiding until the fix lands is not restraint; it is management.

**The SME dismissal.** The FDE reads the SME's "the model missed a nuance" as a familiarity complaint and dismisses it. It was archetype-A-type-(i) — a real quality gap — and the FDE has just thrown away the most valuable eval signal of the month. Replace with: assuming (i) first, always. Every SME complaint gets a trace investigation before it gets a diagnosis of "familiarity cost" or "role threat." The trace investigation costs the FDE thirty minutes; the premature political diagnosis costs the FDE the SME.

**The end-run around IT.** The FDE, frustrated by a slow IT review, goes to the sponsor and asks the sponsor to "unblock" IT. The sponsor pushes IT. IT remembers. For the rest of the pilot, every IT touchpoint is adversarial because the FDE established that IT's role in the pilot is to be managed around. Replace with: the working session, the IT champion, the standing-observer invitation. The one legitimate sponsor-escalation path on IT review is when IT review itself is in bad faith — a political opposition inside IT that will not resolve at the working-session level. That escalation is rare and is still routed through the FDE's own manager first, not directly to the sponsor; the sponsor hears a joint narrative, not a bilateral complaint.

**The "we'll make them love it" assumption.** The FDE believes that once the demo lands with the VP, the resisting SME will fall in line because the VP's endorsement will carry them. SMEs do not work for VPs in the way the FDE's mental model suggests, and the SME's quiet non-adoption after the VP's endorsement can quietly kill a pilot while the sponsor still believes it is healthy. Replace with: the SME is a first-class stakeholder, not a secondary adopter. The exec demo is a parallel track to the SME engagement, not a substitute for it.

**The cheerleader who hides yellow.** The weekly review runs green, the exec brief runs green, the FDE knows two of the week's KPIs are actually yellow and one is on the edge of red. The resisting IT reviewer finds the yellow through their own query of the telemetry data and the FDE has lost the relationship. See [Chapter 04](./04-weekly-pilot-review-cadence.md) on RAG-with-rationale. Replace with: honest colour, with rationale. The incentive to hide yellow intensifies when the pilot is under schedule pressure or when the sponsor has been hearing green for weeks; those are exactly the moments at which hiding yellow does the most damage.

**The escalation without warning.** The FDE sends the escalation email to the sponsor without the Thursday 1-on-1 preview and without the Friday internal-review preview with the `ai-engineering-manager`. The sponsor and the manager both read the email cold. Both of them lose a degree of trust in the FDE's judgement regardless of whether the escalation's content was correct. Replace with: socialise first, write second. The written note exists to formalise what both leaders have already heard verbally; it is the record, not the news.

**The silent withdrawal.** The FDE, worn down by the resisting SME, stops inviting the SME to the weekly review. The SME notices. The SME's director notices. The FDE has just confirmed, structurally, that the pilot was happy to proceed without the SME's expertise — which is exactly the role threat the SME suspected. Replace with: keep the invitation open, keep the pre-meeting 1-on-1 on the calendar, name the disengagement if it persists. The invitation is cheap to keep open; the message of withdrawal is expensive to recover from.

### A ninth anti-pattern worth naming

**The CSM detour.** The FDE, seeing the sponsor relationship fray, routes resistance concerns through the `customer-success-manager` rather than to the sponsor directly or to the FDE's own manager. The CSM is relationship-oriented and often sympathetic, which is why the detour is tempting; but the CSM does not own the pilot's technical escalation path, and routing resistance through them dilutes the signal, slows the response, and puts the CSM in a position they were not hired for. See [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-fde-discipline-and-position/04-adjacent-customer-facing-roles.md). The CSM is informed; the CSM is not the owner.

## A quick reference — the resistance posture in one page

If the chapter collapses to a single page, the FDE should hold three things in working memory.

1. Resistance is information. The SME's "the model missed a nuance" is signal, not noise. The IT reviewer's "we need another three weeks" is often correct. The department's silence is a political read the FDE should surface, not try to resolve alone.
2. The stance is radical transparency plus pragmatic respect — both structural, neither optional. Transparency is the practice of never surprising a stakeholder with a failure that affects them. Respect is the practice of taking the resisting party's craft seriously enough to argue with them on evidence.
3. The move inventory for each archetype is finite and rehearsable. Pre-meeting 1-on-1; co-design session; failure walkthrough; explicit non-goal; respect-signalling vocabulary; shadow session; retrospective invite; working session; IT champion; standing-observer invitation; sponsor + director conversation; escalation to both chains with socialised preview. The FDE's job is to know which move the current situation needs, not to invent a move from scratch under pressure.

## Summary

- Customer-side resistance is a feature of the pilot, not a bug. Domain experts protect quality; organisations protect their workflows; IT protects the enterprise. The FDE's job is to read the resistance correctly, not to make it go away.
- The FDE's stance is radical transparency plus pragmatic respect — both structural, neither optional under schedule pressure. Transparency without respect is cold; respect without transparency is theatre.
- Three archetypes carry the chapter. (A) The SME who prefers the legacy workflow: assume a real quality gap first; if not, name the switching tax; if not, surface the role threat to the sponsor. (B) The department that reads the pilot as a threat: surface the political read to the sponsor and request the sponsor + director conversation. (C) The IT review that feels adversarial: working session, mod-108 one-pager, find the champion, invite the reviewer to the weekly review as a standing observer.
- Specific techniques: the pre-meeting 1-on-1 with the SME lead (standing, never skipped); co-design sessions (SME as shared author); the failure walkthrough (before the SME finds it); the explicit non-goal (name what the pilot will not replace); respect-signalling moves (vocabulary, artefact citation, deferral on domain calls).
- Escalation after 4–6 weeks of persistent resistance despite good-faith engagement. Routed to the sponsor and the FDE's `ai-engineering-manager` in parallel, socialised in 1-on-1s first, named in a short written note with a specific next-step request.
- Eight anti-patterns to catch: the FDE as salesperson, the hidden failure mode, the SME dismissal, the end-run around IT, the "we'll make them love it" assumption, the cheerleader who hides yellow, the escalation without warning, the silent withdrawal.
